# Terrace Layout Guide
## Bucharest 12th-Floor Balcony (S-W, 36sqm Z-terrace)

---

## The Z-Shape and Why It Matters

The terrace is Z-shaped, which creates three distinct microclimates within the same 36sqm space. A flat rectangular terrace has essentially uniform conditions throughout. The Z-shape gives you a windward exposed zone, a transitional mid-section, and a sheltered corner — each suitable for different types of plants.

The key insight: stop thinking of this as one uniform space and start treating it as three different growing environments. The right plant in the wrong zone will struggle. The right plant in the right zone can thrive even on a 12th-floor S-W terrace with no windbreakers.

---

## Zone 1: Windward Edge (Exposed, Full Sun, High Wind)

**Plants**: Pinus mugo (80L pot), Juniperus horizontalis, Juniperus squamata 'Blue Star', Festuca glauca, Stipa tenuissima

**Conditions**: Maximum sun exposure (S-W direct sun from late morning through evening), highest wind speeds, lowest humidity. In July and August this zone receives the full force of 40°C heat and drying wind simultaneously.

**Why these plants work here**:

Pinus mugo is the anchor specimen. It is rated to USDA zone 2-3, tolerates full blasting sun, handles constant wind without defoliating, and in an 80L double-wall pot has enough root mass and thermal buffering to handle summer heat. Its dense branching also provides physical wind interruption for everything immediately behind it.

Low junipers and grasses are the workhorses of the windward edge. They stay under 50-60cm tall, presenting a low profile that reduces wind resistance. Their root systems tolerate alternating drought and watering. They look deliberately naturalistic and structured, which suits the exposed position.

Stipa tenuissima is rated USDA 7 — technically marginal for this terrace. It is included here because its wind tolerance is exceptionally high (it evolved in exposed steppes) and it is inexpensive to replace if a hard winter kills it. Plant it in an exposed position where it can catch wind and create movement, which is its main ornamental quality.

**What NOT to plant here**: Any large-leaved, high-moisture plant. Hydrangea, Magnolia, and Japanese maple in this position would suffer severe scorch and wind damage within weeks.

## Zone 2: Mid-Section Transition (Partial Protection, Full-to-Partial Sun)

**Plants**: Campsis radicans (wall climber), Lavender row, Sedum, Rosa rugosa, Lonicera (climbing), Parthenocissus (wall coverage), Fallopia baldschuanica (wall climber)

**Conditions**: Receives full S-W sun but with slightly reduced wind speed due to Zone 1 plants and the Z-corner. The building wall section runs through this zone, providing climbing surfaces and winter thermal mass.

**Campsis radicans** runs up the building wall. It is vigorous enough to handle this terrace's conditions after establishment and its trumpet flowers in July-August are one of the most striking features possible on a hot balcony. Prune hard (to 2-3 buds) every March — it flowers on new wood.

**Lavender row** is positioned here for full sun with slightly better protection than Zone 1. Use Lavandula angustifolia only ('Hidcote', 'Munstead') — see lavender-post-mortem.md for full details. Group 2-3 plants together in individual well-draining pots, not shared trays.

**Sedum** fills gaps between pots, can spill over pot edges, and requires almost no attention.

**Climbing plants on the building wall** (Lonicera, Parthenocissus, Fallopia): the building wall provides the attachment surface. Use trellis panels fixed to the wall (not permanent structural attachment, but hook-and-eye fixings or tension wire systems) or train along existing wall features. The wall also provides insulation and stability for the pot bases. Tie in stems regularly — wind on a 12th floor dislodges untied climbers.

## Zone 3: Shaded Z-Corner (Sheltered, Partial-to-Full Shade)

**Plants**: Hedera helix (ivy, climbing), Taxus baccata, Bergenia, Hydrangea

**Conditions**: This inside corner of the Z receives significantly less direct sun — morning light only, or filtered light, depending on exact geometry. Wind is deflected by the building wall and the structure of the Z-corner itself. It is the most protected spot on the entire terrace.

**Why the Z-corner is the most valuable microclimate**:
- Wind shadow: the geometry of the corner physically deflects airflow
- Reduced direct sun: plants that cannot tolerate afternoon S-W blasting sun survive here
- Building wall thermal mass: two walls in an inside corner means double the thermal benefit in winter
- Proximity to drainage (typically near door): easy access for watering

**Hedera helix** climbs the walls of this corner, providing year-round greenery, insulation for the wall surfaces, and habitat. English ivy tolerates deep shade and wind once established. Train upward with small hooks.

**Taxus baccata** (yew) is one of the most shade-tolerant evergreens available. Slow-growing, extremely long-lived, tolerates pot culture well. Position in the deepest shade of the corner.

**Bergenia** provides large bold leaves and spring flowers. A true shade-lover that also tolerates cold well. Good ground-cover-style plant at the base of the corner.

**Hydrangea**: The sheltered corner is the only viable position for Hydrangea on this terrace. Even here, Hydrangea macrophylla may struggle — the flower buds are frost-sensitive and can be killed by late frosts in March-April. Consider Hydrangea paniculata instead, which flowers on new wood and is more forgiving.

## Baby Plant Priority Placement

When establishing new plants, position them based on their target mature position — but also consider their current vulnerability.

```
SCORCHING SUN          TRANSITION              SHADED Z-CORNER
(Zone 1, exposed)      (Zone 2, mid-section)   (Zone 3, sheltered)

Fig tree               Magnolia Susan          Japanese maple
Olive tree             Lonicera (climbing)     English ivy (climbing)
(Lavender retry)       Clematis (pot in shade,
                        climbs to sun)
```

**Fig tree** goes in Zone 1 or the warmest part of Zone 2 once established. Fig loves heat, tolerates wind with sufficient water, and produces better fruit with maximum sun exposure. In year one, consider starting it in Zone 2 and moving to Zone 1 in year two after roots establish.

**Olive tree** is a full-sun, heat-tolerant plant that belongs in Zone 1 or Zone 2. It can handle wind at full size. In year one, shelter it slightly during the worst summer heat weeks.

**Magnolia Susan** goes in the transition zone (Zone 2, slightly shaded side). It needs morning sun and afternoon shade in a Bucharest summer. Too much direct afternoon sun scorches the leaves. Too little sun and it will not flower well. The transition zone at the S-W edge of the Z-corner is ideal.

**Japanese maple** goes in Zone 3, the shaded corner. Full afternoon sun in a Bucharest summer will scorch the delicate leaves badly even with all the correct soil and pot systems. Morning light plus afternoon shade is the correct regime. This is not negotiable for this species on this terrace.

**Clematis** has a specific requirement: cool roots, climbing shoot. The pot sits in shade (Zone 3 or the shaded side of Zone 2) while the stems are trained upward toward the sun. This is not a compromise — it is exactly how clematis grows best. A clematis with hot roots in full sun will struggle even with correct watering.

**Lonicera** (climbing honeysuckle) is more tolerant than clematis. It can go in Zone 2 on the building wall, receiving partial sun. Vigorous once established.

**English ivy** belongs in Zone 3 as a wall climber in the sheltered corner.

## The Layout Logic: Rules Behind the Diagram

**Rule 1: Windward edges get indestructible low plants.**
The plants at the windward boundary are the terrace's defense. They take the worst conditions so everything behind them has a better chance. These plants need to be tough enough to ask for no help: low-profile (less wind resistance), drought-tolerant (survive if you miss a watering), cold-hardy well beyond what the terrace can throw at them. Pinus mugo and Juniperus horizontalis are almost impossible to kill here.

**Rule 2: Z-corners create wind shadows — use them for delicates.**
The inside corners of the Z-shape are not just aesthetically interesting — they are physically sheltered. Turbulent wind does not penetrate inside corners the way it does exposed faces. Temperature in the corners is more stable. This is where the Japanese maple, the ivy, and the Hydrangea survive. Do not waste these positions on tough plants that do not need the shelter.

**Rule 3: The building wall provides winter thermal mass — cluster everything against it December through February.**
This is the most important winter action. The building wall's stored heat is the difference between plants surviving a -15°C night and dying. The layout should already have many pots near the building wall. In winter, move every pot as close to the wall as possible, even temporarily breaking up the Zone 1/2/3 arrangement. The arrangement is for the growing season. Winter survival is about the wall.

**Rule 4: Climbers belong on the building wall, not on freestanding structures.**
Freestanding trellises or stakes experience the same extreme conditions as everything else on the terrace. The building wall is structurally stable, thermally beneficial, and physically attached to the building — it will not blow over. All climbers should be trained against the building wall or attached trellis panels fixed to it.

**Rule 5: Group pots together, never isolate.**
An isolated pot on an exposed 12th-floor terrace faces worst-case conditions from all sides. Grouped pots share humidity, reduce wind exposure for each other, and create a small local microclimate. The difference between a single isolated pot and a group of 5 pots is significant for plant health. Arrange pots so they are close to each other, with taller pots sheltering shorter ones.
