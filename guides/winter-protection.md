# Winter Protection Guide
## Bucharest 12th-Floor Balcony (S-W, 36sqm Z-terrace)
## USDA Zone 6b | -10°C to -20°C winters

---

## The Core Problem: Pots Freeze Faster Than Ground

A plant rated USDA 7b may die in a USDA 6b pot garden even though it would survive in a USDA 7b ground garden. This is not a labeling error — it is physics.

In the ground, soil has enormous thermal mass. The frost line in Bucharest reaches 60-80cm, meaning roots below that depth never freeze even in the coldest winters. In a pot, the entire root system is exposed on all sides to ambient air temperature. A plant in a 40L pot experiences cold as if it were planted in a shallow raised bed with no insulation — temperatures inside the pot can drop to air temperature or lower with wind chill on an elevated terrace.

The result: treat all potted plants as if they are one USDA hardiness zone less cold-hardy than their rating suggests, and insulate accordingly.

---

## November: Wrap the Pots (Not the Plants)

Wrap pots, not plants. Wrapping plant foliage traps moisture, encourages fungal disease, and can actually cause more freeze damage in conifers and evergreens by raising humidity and reducing airflow. What needs insulation is the root system — and roots live in the pot.

### Materials

**Bubble wrap**: Excellent insulation. Wrap 2-3 layers around the outer pot, securing with tape or string. The air bubbles provide significant insulation. Cheap, available at any hardware store.

**Coconut coir matting (cocomat)**: Natural fiber mats, 2-3cm thick. Wrap and tie around pots. Better UV stability than bubble wrap, more attractive, biodegrades cleanly. Available at garden centers (Hornbach, Leroy Merlin).

**Horticultural fleece**: Wrap around both pot and stem base for extra-sensitive plants. Not sufficient as the only insulation for pots — use in addition to bubble wrap or coir, not instead of.

**Hessian/burlap**: Similar to coir, traditional, effective for larger pots.

### Wrapping Method

1. Do the first wrap in early-to-mid November, before the first hard frost.
2. Wrap from the base of the pot to just above the soil line — cover the entire outer pot surface.
3. Secure with string, zip ties, or waterproof tape. Wind will dislodge loose wrapping.
4. Do not cover drainage holes — they must remain open for excess water drainage and air circulation.
5. Top-dress the soil with extra mulch (8-10cm) for additional root zone insulation.

Remove wrapping in mid-March when nighttime temperatures reliably stay above -3°C.

## December through February: Group Pots Against Building Wall

Move all pots as close as physically possible to the building wall between December and February. The building wall is the most important winter asset on this terrace.

### Why the Building Wall Matters

The concrete and masonry building wall absorbs internal building heat throughout the winter. Surface temperature of an exterior building wall on the sheltered side typically stays 5-8°C warmer than ambient air temperature on cold nights. In a -15°C night, the area within 30-50cm of the building wall may stay at -7°C to -10°C. For a plant rated to -15°C, this can be the difference between survival and death.

The wall also blocks wind from one direction, reducing wind chill on the pot root zones.

### Arrangement

- Push all pots to within 20-30cm of the building wall. The closer, the better.
- Largest, heaviest pots (Pinus mugo, Olive, Fig) against the wall directly.
- Smaller pots grouped tightly in front of and beside the larger ones. The grouping means they share thermal mass with each other.
- Leave no single pot isolated in the middle of the terrace in winter.
- The Z-shape inside corners — which are already more sheltered from wind — are the best winter positions.

---

## Elevate Pots: Drainage Holes Must Not Freeze Shut

Keep all pots on feet throughout winter. If a drainage hole freezes shut and the pot receives rain or snowmelt, water accumulates inside the pot with nowhere to go. This sitting water then freezes, expanding and potentially cracking the pot and creating ice blocks around the roots.

Pot feet also prevent pots from bonding to the terrace surface through freeze-thaw cycles, which can crack both the pot base and the terrace tiles.

Minimum foot height: 2-3cm. Same spacers used for the double-wall system work year-round.

## Pruning Timing: Never Prune in Autumn

Autumn pruning is one of the most common mistakes in balcony gardening. It feels productive — the plant looks tidy going into winter. But it is actively harmful.

**Why autumn pruning causes damage**:
- Fresh cut surfaces cannot harden before frost. The wound tissue freezes and dies, potentially allowing disease entry into the main stem.
- Pruning signals the plant to produce new growth. Any new growth flush in October or November is soft, frost-tender, and will be killed by the first hard frost.
- Removing foliage reduces the plant's ability to photosynthesize and store carbohydrates needed for winter survival.

### Correct Pruning Windows

| Plant type | Prune when |
|---|---|
| Conifers (Pinus mugo, Junipers) | Late February to mid-March, before new growth |
| Deciduous trees and shrubs (Maple, Fig) | March, once completely dormant but before bud break |
| Climbers (Campsis, Clematis, Lonicera, Fallopia) | Late February to March. Campsis can be cut hard. Clematis: depends on group |
| Roses (Rosa rugosa) | March, when buds start to swell |
| Evergreen shrubs (Taxus, Buxus, Euonymus) | Late March to April for shaping |
| Lavender | Early spring only — never cut into old wood |
| Hydrangea | March, based on species (some flower on old wood — check type before cutting) |

The rule for this terrace: if in doubt, wait until March.

## Plants That Need Indoor Shelter Below -10°C

### Olive (Olea europaea)

Olive trees tolerate down to about -7°C to -10°C when mature and established. Below -10°C, serious dieback or death is likely. On this terrace, -10°C to -20°C winters mean outdoor olive survival is marginal even with maximum protection.

**Outdoor-only strategy (risky)**: Pot wrap with double-layer bubble wrap + coir mat, pushed against building wall, additional fleece wrap over the canopy during extreme cold events (below -8°C). This may work in mild winters (-10°C minimum) but is a gamble.

**Safe strategy**: Move the olive pot indoors to a cool, bright location (unheated garage with window, cool stairwell with natural light, or cool bright room) when temperatures drop below -5°C. Bring back out in late March.

Mediterranean plants do not need warm indoor conditions — they prefer a cool dormant period. An unheated but frost-free space (2-8°C) with some light is ideal.

**Critical**: Keep olive almost dry indoors. Water once every 3-4 weeks at most.

### Fig (Ficus carica) — First and Second Years

Adult established figs (3+ years, substantial root system) can sometimes survive Bucharest winters with maximum pot insulation. Young figs in their first and second years cannot. The root system is too small to buffer cold, and the above-ground wood has not hardened fully.

Move young figs indoors before the first hard frost (before temperatures drop below -3°C to -5°C). An unheated cool room (2-10°C), minimal light acceptable as the plant is fully deciduous, minimal watering. Bring back out in late March to April.

From the third year onwards, assess based on the individual plant's vigor and the severity of recent winters.

## Mediterranean Plants in Winter: Wet + Cold = Death

This combination kills lavender, olive, and other Mediterranean plants more reliably than cold alone.

Mediterranean plants evolved in climates with cool dry winters. Their physiology assumes frozen or near-frozen roots will be dry. Wet cold soil prevents cellular membranes from managing ice crystal formation properly, causing cell rupture at temperatures the plant would survive if dry.

**Rule for Mediterranean plants (Lavender, Olive, Sedum, Sempervivum, Festuca, Stipa) in winter**:
- Keep almost completely dry from November through February.
- Do not water unless soil has been completely dry for more than 2-3 weeks AND the weather is forecast to remain above freezing.
- Remove from any shared tray system for winter — they must not have continuous moisture supplied by capillary mat.
- Ensure pot drainage is perfect and drainage holes are not blocked by debris or ice.
- If the plant sits in a sunny position and gets some winter sun warmth, check the drainage is functioning and the reservoir (if using double-wall system) is empty for winter.
