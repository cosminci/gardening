# Pot Systems Guide
## Bucharest 12th-Floor Balcony (S-W, 36sqm Z-terrace)

---

## Why Standard Pots Fail Here

A plain pot on this terrace is a death trap in July. Direct sun on dark plastic raises root zone temperature to 50-60°C. Wind pulls moisture out faster than roots can absorb it. A plant sitting in a saucer drowns when rain hits and bakes when it doesn't. Every system below exists to solve these specific problems.

---

## System A: Individual Double-Wall Insulated Pot

### How It Works

Each plant lives in an **inner pot** (has drainage holes) that sits elevated inside a larger **outer pot** (no drainage holes). The outer pot acts as a reservoir. A cotton wick threads from the soil through a drainage hole and into the reservoir water. An air gap between the two pots insulates the root zone from the outer wall temperature.

```
┌─────────────────────────────┐
│  OUTER POT (NO drain holes) │
│   ┌──────────────────────┐  │
│   │     INNER POT        │  │
│   │   (HAS drain holes)  │  │
│   └──────┬──┬──┬─────────┘  │
│          │  │  │  ← FEET    │  2-3cm
│   ▓▓▓▓▓▓│▓▓│▓▓│▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓  │  ← wick
│   ░░░░░RESERVOIR░░░░░░░░░░  │  5-8cm water
└──────────────────────────────┘
```

### Why This Is NOT "Wet Feet"

"Wet feet" means roots sitting in stagnant water with no oxygen — the classic cause of root rot. This system avoids it through three mechanisms:

1. **Air gap**: The inner pot never touches standing water. The 2-3cm spacers guarantee a permanent oxygen layer between the water surface and the pot bottom.
2. **Capillary action**: The cotton wick draws only as much water as the soil needs. When soil is moist, capillary pull is weaker. When soil dries, pull increases. The plant regulates its own intake.
3. **Self-regulating reservoir**: The plant takes what it needs when it needs it. In peak summer heat it may drain the reservoir in 2 days. In cool overcast weather the same water lasts a week. You refill the outer pot, not the soil.

### Construction Details

**Outer pot**: Any material, no drainage holes. Must be at least 6-8cm wider than the inner pot on all sides to allow the air gap. Dark-colored outer pots are acceptable because the inner pot's air gap prevents that heat from reaching roots.

**Inner pot**: Ceramic, terracotta, or fabric (see Fabric Pots section). Must have drainage holes. Size the inner pot for the plant's mature root ball.

**Feet/spacers**: Terracotta feet, cut sections of 3cm PVC pipe, or stacked ceramic tiles. Height: 2-3cm minimum. Must be stable and not block drainage holes.

**Wick**: Natural cotton rope, 6-8mm thick. Cut 30-40cm length. Thread one end through a drainage hole in the inner pot, coil it in the soil 5-10cm deep. The other end hangs down into the reservoir. Use 1 wick per 20cm pot diameter (2 wicks for pots over 40cm).

**Reservoir depth**: Fill outer pot to 5-8cm water level. Never let it exceed the bottom of the inner pot. Refill when empty or nearly empty — don't top up daily.

### Best For

- All large single specimens (Japanese maple, Magnolia, Fig, Olive, Pinus mugo)
- Any plant where root zone temperature control is critical
- Plants that need consistent moisture (Campsis, Clematis)

## System B: Shared Tray with Capillary Mat

### How It Works

Multiple smaller pots sit on feet on top of a **capillary mat** inside a shallow tray (3-5cm deep). The tray holds 1-2cm of water. The capillary mat wicks water upward to the pot feet, and from there through drainage holes into soil. Each pot self-regulates independently.

### Construction Details

**Tray**: Plastic or metal, 3-5cm deep. Size to hold a cluster of pots. Must be level — even 1cm slope causes uneven water distribution.

**Capillary mat**: Horticultural felt or purpose-made capillary matting. Cut to tray size. The mat holds water like a sponge and releases it slowly upward.

**Feet**: Each pot needs 1-2cm feet so drainage holes contact the mat without pots sitting flat (flat = anaerobic mat = rot).

**Water level**: 1-2cm in tray. The mat soaks this up and holds it. Top up when the mat starts to dry (every 2-4 days in summer, longer in spring/autumn).

### Why Self-Regulating

Each pot draws from the mat at its own rate. A thirsty Lonicera will pull more than a dry-tolerant Sedum sitting next to it. The mat acts as a buffer, smoothing out demand spikes.

### Best For

- Groups of smaller pots (herbs, Sedum, Sempervivum, small Festuca)
- Propagation pots and first-year baby plants
- Plants with similar water needs clustered together

### Caution

Do NOT mix Mediterranean drought-tolerant plants (Lavender, Sedum) in the same tray as thirsty moisture-lovers (Hydrangea, Bergenia). Their water needs conflict. Separate trays per plant group.

## Fabric Pots as Inner Pots

### Benefits

Fabric pots are outstanding inner pots for large specimens. The porous walls allow air pruning — when roots reach the wall they are pruned by air contact instead of circling. This forces the plant to produce dense lateral roots, resulting in a much healthier root system than hard-walled pots allow.

### Critical Rule: Fabric Bottom Must NOT Touch Water

Fabric wicks moisture aggressively. If the fabric base contacts the reservoir water, the capillary pull is uncontrolled and roots stay permanently wet. Use **3-4cm spacers** — taller than the standard 2-3cm — to guarantee a clear gap between fabric and water surface.

### Best Plants for Fabric Inner Pots

- Japanese maple (Acer palmatum)
- Magnolia Susan
- Fig (Ficus carica)
- Clematis

### Why Fabric Pots Fail as Standalone Pots on This Terrace

Fabric pots used without an outer pot are unsuitable for this specific location:

- **Extreme evaporation**: The breathable walls that make fabric pots excellent for roots also lose moisture rapidly. On a 40°C day with strong wind, a fabric pot can lose lethal amounts of water in hours.
- **Zero insulation**: Root zone temperature in a standalone fabric pot can reach 55-60°C in direct sun. Most plants cannot survive this.
- **UV degradation**: Fabric pots degrade within 2-3 seasons in direct sun. Structural failure during a storm is a real hazard on a 12th floor.
- **Wind instability**: Empty or dry fabric pots are easily blown over or moved by strong wind gusts.

Always use fabric pots as inner pots inside a solid outer pot with a reservoir.

## Commercial Option: Lechuza Self-Watering Planters

Lechuza planters are a premium commercial implementation of System A. They include a hard inner planter, an outer reservoir, a fill tube with water level indicator, and a wicking substrate. They are reliable, attractive, and tested for long-term use.

**Advantages**: Professional quality, water level visible through indicator, available in large sizes (Cubico 50, Balconera 100), UV-stable.

**Disadvantages**: Expensive (150-400 RON per unit), limited size options for very large plants, proprietary substrate recommended but not required.

**Where to buy in Romania**: Dedeman, Leroy Merlin, Hornbach, Amazon.de (ships to Romania).

Lechuza is a valid alternative for any plant that would otherwise use System A.

## Automated Drip Irrigation

Manual watering is unsustainable in peak summer, especially during holidays. A basic automated system is strongly recommended.

**Recommended system**: Gardena MicroDrip System (or equivalent Claber/Rain Bird).

**Approximate cost**: 300-500 RON for a 20-pot system including:
- Tap connector with timer (Gardena 01266-20 or similar): 150-200 RON
- Master unit and distribution tubing: 80-120 RON
- Drip heads and stakes (20 pcs): 60-100 RON
- Extension tubing and connectors: 40-80 RON

**Setup notes for this terrace**:
- Use pressure-compensating drip heads (not standard) — the elevation change across the Z-shape terrace can cause uneven flow
- Set timer to 6:00-6:30 AM (before peak heat)
- Run 5-10 minutes daily in summer, adjust by season
- The drip system supplements but does not replace the reservoir systems — fill reservoirs weekly, let drip handle daily soil surface moisture for non-reservoir pots
