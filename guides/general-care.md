# General Care Calendar
## Bucharest 12th-Floor Balcony (S-W, 36sqm Z-terrace)

---

## Watering by Season

### The Finger Test Rule

Before every watering decision, push your finger 3-4cm into the soil. If it feels damp, do not water. If it feels dry, water. This rule overrides all calendar schedules. The schedules below are defaults — the finger test is reality.

For reservoir pots (System A double-wall): check the reservoir level, not just the soil surface. A full reservoir with dry-feeling surface soil is normal — the wick is working. An empty reservoir with dry soil is urgent.

### Summer (June — August)

- **Normal conditions**: Water daily, early morning, 6:00-7:00 AM before peak heat begins.
- **Heat wave conditions (above 38°C for 3+ consecutive days)**: Water twice daily — early morning and again at 10:00 PM when temperatures drop. Never water midday (water droplets on leaves in direct sun can scorch, and water in hot soil evaporates before reaching roots).
- **Reservoir pots**: Refill reservoirs every 2-3 days in normal summer, daily during heat waves.
- **Mediterranean plants (Lavender, Sedum, Olive)**: Still every 2-3 days even in summer. They want to dry between waterings.
- **Volume**: Water thoroughly until water runs from drainage holes (or reservoir is filled). Shallow watering encourages surface roots that are more vulnerable to heat.

### Spring and Autumn (March-May, September-November)

- Water every 2-3 days as a default.
- Adjust based on finger test and weather — a cool rainy week may require no watering at all.
- Reservoir pots: top up as needed, roughly weekly.
- Reduce frequency in November as temperatures drop and plant metabolism slows.

### Winter (December — February)

- Water every 10-14 days on frost-free days only.
- **Critical rule**: Never water when soil is frozen or when nighttime temperature is below 0°C and dropping. Water in frozen soil expands, rupturing root cells and cracking pots.
- Water at midday on mild days (minimum 3°C air temperature).
- Mediterranean plants (Olive, Lavender): keep almost completely dry. Water once per month at most if there has been no rain.
- Deciduous plants (maple, fig, fallopia): almost no water needed — they are dormant. Light watering once every 2-3 weeks if soil is completely dry.
- Evergreens (Pinus mugo, Taxus, Junipers, Buxus, Euonymus): water every 2-3 weeks on mild days. They continue slow transpiration even in winter.
- Remove saucers and do not use tray system in winter — standing cold water accelerates root damage.

## Feeding Schedule

### March — Slow-Release Base

Apply slow-release granular fertilizer at the start of the growing season. Work lightly into the top 2-3cm of soil or scatter under mulch.

- Use a balanced or slightly high-nitrogen formula for foliage plants and structure plants (e.g., Osmocote Exact 5-6 month, 15-9-12 or similar).
- Use a low-nitrogen, high-potassium formula for flowering and fruiting plants (Fig, Rosa rugosa, Campsis, Magnolia) — less leafy growth, more flowers.
- Follow pack dosage. Do not over-apply — in a pot, excess fertilizer has nowhere to go and can burn roots.
- Acid-loving plants (Japanese maple, Magnolia): use a fertilizer specifically formulated for ericaceous plants or with added trace elements including iron.
- Do NOT apply slow-release to Mediterranean drought-tolerant plants (Lavender, Sedum, Sempervivum, Stipa) — they are adapted to poor soil and excess fertility causes soft floppy growth that is more vulnerable to winter damage.

### May through September — Liquid Supplements

- Supplement slow-release with liquid fertilizer every 2 weeks at **half the recommended strength**.
- Half strength applied twice as often is gentler and more effective than full strength applied monthly.
- Use balanced liquid feed (NPK roughly equal) for foliage and structural plants.
- Switch to high-potassium liquid feed (tomato feed works well) from July onwards for flowering and fruiting plants. High potassium hardens growth, improves flower and fruit production, and prepares plants for winter.
- Apply liquid feed in the morning when watering, never in peak heat.
- Acid-loving plants: use an acidic liquid fertilizer (Miracid, Substral Orchid or Ericaceous) through the season.

### October — Wind Down

- Stop all liquid feeding from October.
- The slow-release granules will continue to supply trace nutrients at a reduced rate as temperatures drop and release slows naturally.
- Do not apply any fertilizer that promotes new growth — any flush of soft new growth in October or November will be killed by the first frost and can open the plant to disease.

### November through February — Zero

- No feeding at all.
- Plants are dormant or near-dormant. They cannot use nutrients and excess salts sit in the soil.

## Wind Management Without Windbreakers

No structural windbreakers are permitted on this terrace. Wind management is achieved through plant placement and the inherent geometry of the Z-shape.

### Mapping the Wind Shadows

The Z-shape terrace creates natural shelter zones. Stand on the terrace in a strong wind and identify:
- Where the wind hits first and hardest (typically the SW-facing corner at the open end of the Z)
- Where eddies and calm zones form (inside corners of the Z-shape)
- Where the building wall provides a thermal and physical buffer

Mark these zones — windward exposed, transition, and sheltered — and place plants accordingly (see layout.md for the specific diagram).

### Using Plants as Barriers

- **Windward edges**: Plant the most robust, low-growing, dense plants at the exposed edges. Pinus mugo, low junipers (Juniperus horizontalis, J. squamata 'Blue Star'), and ornamental grasses (Festuca, Stipa) tolerate constant wind and physically interrupt airflow for plants behind them.
- **Wind shadow creation**: A mass of dense low plants 50-60cm tall at the windward edge creates a zone of reduced wind speed extending 3-5x the plant height downwind. Two 60cm junipers side by side create a meaningful calm zone behind them.
- **Mass planting for microclimate**: Cluster pots together rather than spacing them evenly. Pots grouped closely reduce each other's wind exposure and collectively raise local humidity — both are beneficial. Isolated single pots on a windy balcony suffer more than a dense cluster even if the individual plants are the same species.

### Heavy Pots on Edges

- The windward edge positions receive the heaviest pots (80-100L Pinus mugo, large Juniper pots). Weight prevents them from being toppled or shifted.
- Never place lightweight pots (small fabric pots, lightweight plastic) on the wind-exposed perimeter.
- During severe weather events (storms, wind speed warnings), move all non-heavy pots away from the perimeter to the building wall side.

### Protecting Climbers from Wind Damage

Campsis, Clematis, Lonicera, Parthenocissus, and Fallopia on the building wall or trellis must be tied in regularly. Wind whips long untied stems, causing mechanical damage at attachment points and desiccation of exposed growth. Check and add ties every 4-6 weeks during the growing season.

## Summer Heat Protection

### Mulch Every Pot (5cm)

Applying 5cm of mulch to every pot surface in May is the single most effective heat protection measure. It reduces soil surface temperature by up to 15°C and cuts evaporation by 30-50%. Use bark chips for most plants, decorative gravel for Mediterranean Group B plants (bark retains too much moisture for lavender and sedum).

Refresh mulch every spring. Partially decomposed mulch is still beneficial but loses efficiency after one full season.

### Light-Colored Pots

Where possible, use light-colored outer pots (white, cream, light grey, terracotta). Dark pots absorb significantly more solar radiation. On a S-W exposure, a black plastic pot in direct sun reaches temperatures that would kill most roots even with the double-wall system. The double-wall air gap helps, but starting with a light-colored outer pot reduces the problem at the source.

Where you already have dark pots, wrap the outer pot with white or light-colored reflective material (aluminium foil, white burlap, reflective pot wrap) during July and August.

### Moving Heat-Sensitive Plants in July and August

Japanese maple and Magnolia Susan — both Group A acid-loving plants — are the most heat-sensitive specimens on this terrace. In July and August:

- Move their pots to the shaded Z-corner if not already positioned there (see layout.md).
- If no shade is available, position them where they receive morning sun only (before 12:00) and are shaded from the brutal afternoon S-W sun.
- A temporary shade cloth (30-40% shade) rigged over sensitive plants during the worst heat weeks is effective and removable — it is not a structural windbreaker.

### General Rules for Heat Waves (3+ days above 38°C)

- Water twice daily (morning and late evening).
- Check reservoirs daily.
- Move any stressed or newly planted specimens to the most sheltered position.
- Do not fertilize, prune, or repot during heat waves. Plants under heat stress cannot recover from additional physical stress.

## Golden Rules for Baby Plants in Their First Year

Every plant on this terrace was a baby once, and the first year is the highest-risk period. The root system is small, not yet established, and cannot buffer temperature or moisture extremes. First-year plants need extra care regardless of how tough the species is supposed to be.

### Watering: More Often, Smaller Amounts

An established fig tree has a large root system that can buffer some dryout. A first-year fig in a new pot has a fraction of those roots. Water new plants more frequently — every day in summer even if the species is drought-tolerant — but with smaller volumes. The goal is to keep the root zone consistently moist without waterlogging during establishment.

Once the plant shows strong new growth (a sign that roots are establishing), gradually transition to the standard watering regime for the species.

### No Fertilizer for the First 4-6 Weeks

Quality potting mixes contain sufficient nutrients for 4-6 weeks. Adding more fertilizer to a newly planted specimen stresses roots that are already dealing with transplant shock. Wait until the plant shows healthy new growth before starting any feeding program.

After 4-6 weeks: start with half the recommended dose of liquid fertilizer. Build up to the normal schedule over the rest of the first season.

### Temporary Shade Cloth if Scorch Appears

Leaf scorch (brown dry edges or tips, bleached patches on leaves) in a new plant means the sun intensity exceeds what the plant can handle while still establishing roots. A temporary 30-40% shade cloth draped over the plant for 2-3 weeks while it establishes is not a permanent solution — the plant will eventually acclimatize — but it prevents irreversible leaf damage in the critical early weeks.

### No Pruning in the First Year

Pruning directs plant energy to produce new growth from cut points. A first-year plant needs all its energy for root establishment. Any pruning redirects that energy to shoots instead of roots, slowing establishment and potentially making the plant more vulnerable to stress.

Exception: remove any dead, diseased, or clearly damaged material immediately regardless of year.

### Every First-Year Pot Wrapped for First Winter

An established plant with a developed root system can handle -10°C to -15°C with pot wrapping. A first-year plant with a small root system in the same pot cannot buffer cold to the same degree. Wrap every pot in its first winter (see winter-protection.md), regardless of how hardy the species is rated. The rating assumes an established plant — first-year specimens need extra protection.
